# Health, fitness, nutrition and gym apps — landscape review

**Purpose:** to give the Nurture team a durable, accurate map of the consumer health-app
category, so product and positioning decisions rest on what real products actually do rather
than on marketing claims.

**Scope and limitations:** this review covers consumer apps for food and nutrition tracking,
gym and strength logging, cardio and outdoor sport, sleep and recovery, and the health-data
platforms that connect them. It is a qualitative landscape, not a benchmark. It does not rank
apps by popularity and deliberately does not reproduce store ratings, review counts, download
numbers, user counts, prices or release dates, because figures of this kind change constantly
and must be re-verified against the live store listing before publication. Feature descriptions
reflect each vendor's documented positioning at a product-category level; where a document like
this would normally cite a number, we state the durable fact instead and flag that the figure
needs re-verification.

## Executive answer

There is no single app that is genuinely best at everything. "All-in-one" in this category
almost always means **a bundle of basics**, not best-in-class depth in every area: a strong food
diary with a thin gym logger, or an excellent recovery platform with almost no nutrition tooling.
The depth lives in specialists. A practical combination beats a single subscription:

- **Food and nutrition:** a dedicated tracker (for example MyFitnessPal, Cronometer, YAZIO or
  Fitia) for the diary, macros and micronutrients.
- **Training:** a dedicated logger (Hevy, Strong) or a coached platform (Fitbod, Freeletics) for
  strength, plus a dedicated app (Strava) for outdoor cardio.
- **Recovery and the data hub:** a wearable and its companion app (Garmin Connect, WHOOP, Oura),
  with Apple Health or Android Health Connect as the neutral hub underneath.

Nurture's opportunity is not to win every column of the comparison matrix. It is to be the layer
that connects these jobs to a person's real life, explains *why* a plan changes, and never turns
tracking into a source of shame.

## What "all" should mean

A genuinely "all-in-one" product would have to do all of these jobs well. Each is a distinct
discipline with its own depth.

| Job | What a strong product must do |
|---|---|
| Food diary | Log meals by meal slot, servings and portions; edit and delete; totals per meal and per day. |
| AI food entry | Estimate a meal from a photo or description, label it as an estimate, and let the user correct it before saving. |
| Calories/macros | Show energy, protein, carbohydrate and fat; support goals without shaming; handle servings maths correctly. |
| Micronutrients | Track vitamins, minerals, fibre and other micros against reference intakes, with honest gaps where data is missing. |
| Recipes/meal plans | Search and save recipes; plan days or weeks; generate a shopping list from what was planned. |
| Hydration | Log water with gentle, dismissible reminders and no rigid universal target. |
| Gym training | Build routines, log sets/reps/weight one at a time, show progress history, support progressive overload. |
| Coached exercise | Adapt sessions to equipment, ability and recovery; explain the reasoning; offer easier and harder options. |
| Outdoor cardio | Record walks, runs and rides with distance, pace and route; show personal history rather than a global ranking. |
| Body progress | Optional weight and measurements, trends, and never a target or a score. |
| Sleep/recovery | Track sleep and readiness as trends, not measurements; connect to a wearable where available. |
| Health-data hub | Read and write health records across devices and apps with per-field permission and duplicate handling. |

"## App profiles

**MyFitnessPal** — the long-standing reference food diary, built on a very large user-contributed
food database with barcode scanning and macro tracking. Suits people who want the widest food
coverage and are comfortable editing crowd-sourced entries. Strongest at diary breadth and
ecosystem integrations; trade-offs are database quality (duplicate and mis-entered foods) and a
premium paywall around some features. It acquired **Cal AI** (announced late 2025); Cal AI
remains a standalone app. Natural limit: it is a logger, not a coached training or recovery
platform.

**Cronometer** — the micronutrient specialist. Tracks vitamins, minerals and other micros against
reference intakes with a reputation for data accuracy. Suits people managing nutrient intake,
restricted diets or clinical-adjacent goals. Trade-offs: a denser interface and a smaller recipe
and social surface. Limit: it will not coach your gym programme or outdoor training.

**MacroFactor** — an algorithm-led nutrition coach that estimates energy expenditure from your
own logged intake and weight trend, then adjusts targets weekly. Suits people who want a
data-driven, non-shaming approach and will log consistently. Trade-off: it is subscription-only
and depends on disciplined logging to be useful. Limit: nutrition-focused, not a full fitness
platform.

**Lifesum** — a habit-oriented diary with diet styles, meal plans and a clean, calm interface.
Suits people who want guided structure and gentle habit nudges. Trade-offs: depth in logging and
micros is less than the specialists; much of the plan content is behind a subscription.

**YAZIO** — a broad diary with recipes, fasting tools and meal plans, strong in European and
German-speaking markets. Suits general weight-management users. Trade-off: a freemium model with
ads and a paywalled pro tier.

**Fitia** — meal-planning and calorie tracking with a strong footprint in Latin America and
Spanish/Portuguese markets, including regional food data. Suits users who want automatic meal
plans built around local food. Trade-off: a paid model and less depth in training.

**Cal AI** — an **AI-first food logger**: photograph a meal, get an estimated calorie and macro
breakdown, confirm or edit, and save. It is a focused logging shortcut, not a complete fitness
platform. The vendor has published an **approximate accuracy figure for photo-based estimates**;
this is a vendor claim rather than an independent audit, and **the figure must be re-verified**
against the live listing and vendor material before publication. Its natural limit is exactly the
category-wide one: a photo estimate is a draft, never a measurement.

**Hevy** — a modern strength-training logger with routines, live set tracking, progress charts and
a social feed. Suits lifters who want a clean, capable free tier. Trade-off: it is training-only;
food and recovery live elsewhere.

**Strong** — a straightforward, well-liked strength logger with routines and history; some
features sit behind a subscription. Suits lifters who want simplicity. Limit: strength logging
only.

**Fitbod** — generates and adapts strength workouts based on equipment, history and recovery
signals. Suits people who want programming done for them. Trade-off: subscription-based and
algorithm-driven, so it can feel less transparent than a hand-built plan.

**Freeletics** — coached bodyweight, HIIT and running training with progressive programmes and a
subscription. Suits people training without a gym. Trade-off: less granular nutrition and lifting
depth.

**Strava** — the social home for running, cycling and outdoor sport, with segments, routes and
challenges. Suits endurance athletes who value a shared feed and group challenges. Trade-off: the
social comparison surface is its strength and its risk; it is not a nutrition or recovery tool.
